MODULE 3 — CONSENSUS PROTOCOLS ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is one of the most important modules for your Monday exam.

If a 10-mark question comes from Unit I, Consensus is one of the strongest possibilities.

Your PPT defines consensus as achieving agreement between distrusting nodes on the final state of data. A consensus mechanism is the set of steps used by nodes to agree on a proposed state/value and determine which transactions are valid.

1. Why Do We Need Consensus?

Let's start from the fundamental problem.

Suppose there are 5 blockchain nodes:

       N1
      /  \
    N2    N3
     \    /
      N4
       |
      N5

Alice sends:

"Send ₹100 to Bob."

All nodes need to eventually agree:

Is this transaction valid?
        ↓
YES or NO
        ↓
What is the final state of the ledger?

But these nodes:

  • Don't necessarily trust each other.
  • May receive messages at different times.
  • May fail.
  • May be malicious.
  • May receive conflicting transactions.

Therefore, simply saying:

"Everyone, please agree"

doesn't work.

We need a consensus mechanism.

2. Definition of Consensus ⭐

Exam definition

Consensus is the process of achieving agreement among distrusting nodes on the final state of data in a distributed system.

This is essentially the definition used in your PPT.

Consensus mechanism

A consensus mechanism is a set of steps followed by nodes in a blockchain to agree on a proposed state or value and determine which transactions are valid.

Simple meaning

Many computers
      ↓
Different views / proposals
      ↓
Consensus mechanism
      ↓
ONE accepted state

3. Why Is Consensus Difficult?

With two computers:

A ↔ B

agreement is relatively easy.

But imagine:

N1
N2
N3
N4
N5
...
N10,000

They need to agree on the same state.

Some could be faulty or malicious.

Your PPT specifically says that reaching agreement becomes challenging when multiple nodes participate in a distributed system.

4. Requirements of Consensus ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is a very likely 5-mark question.

Your PPT gives five requirements:

  1. Agreement
  2. Integrity
  3. Validity
  4. Fault tolerance
  5. Termination

Let's understand each.

4.1 Agreement

All honest nodes should decide on the same value.

Example:

N1 → ACCEPT
N2 → ACCEPT
N3 → ACCEPT
N4 → ACCEPT

They shouldn't have:

N1 → ACCEPT
N2 → REJECT
N3 → ACCEPT
N4 → REJECT

Exam line:

Agreement ensures that all honest nodes reach the same decision.

4.2 Integrity

A node shouldn't make the decision more than once during a single consensus cycle.

For example:

N1 → ACCEPT

It shouldn't later change its decision within that same cycle to:

N1 → REJECT

Your PPT describes integrity as the requirement that no node makes the decision more than once in a single consensus cycle.

4.3 Validity

The agreed value should be based on a value initially proposed by at least one honest node.

In simple terms:

Consensus shouldn't magically produce an unrelated value.

4.4 Fault Tolerance

The system should continue working correctly even if some nodes are:

  • Faulty
  • Malicious
  • Byzantine

Your PPT specifically includes Byzantine nodes in this requirement.

4.5 Termination

Eventually, honest nodes must finish the consensus process and reach a decision.

It shouldn't continue forever:

Vote → Vote → Vote → Vote → ...

Instead:

Proposal
   ↓
Consensus process
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
STOP

Your PPT explicitly includes termination as a consensus requirement.

⭐ Memorize This

For the exam:

A-I-V-F-T
A → Agreement
I → Integrity
V → Validity
F → Fault tolerance
T → Termination

If asked:

"What are the requirements of consensus?"

Immediately write:

Agreement, Integrity, Validity, Fault tolerance, Termination.

5. Types of Faults

Your Consensus PPT divides faults into two important categories:

1. Fail-stop faults

2. Byzantine faults

6. Fail-Stop Fault

A fail-stop fault means the node simply stops functioning.

Example:

N1 → Working
N2 → Working
N3 → CRASH ❌
N4 → Working

The node doesn't necessarily behave maliciously.

It simply stops.

Example

Computer crashes.

Solutions mentioned in your PPT:

  • Paxos
  • RAFT

Don't worry about Paxos/RAFT deeply for this syllabus unless your teacher specifically asks them.

7. Byzantine Fault ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is much more dangerous.

A Byzantine node can behave maliciously/arbitrarily.

Example:

N1 → "Transaction valid"
N2 → "Transaction valid"
N3 → "Transaction INVALID"
N4 → "Transaction valid"

Or even:

To N1: ACCEPT
To N2: REJECT

That's why Byzantine faults are much harder.

Your PPT associates Byzantine faults with malicious behavior, software bugs and data corruption.

8. Fail-Stop vs Byzantine

Very useful 5-mark comparison

Fail-stop fault Byzantine fault
Node stops/crashes Node behaves arbitrarily/maliciously
Easier to handle More difficult
Node generally stops responding May send false/conflicting information
Paxos/RAFT can address fail-stop settings PBFT addresses Byzantine settings
Example: computer crash Example: malicious node sends conflicting messages

Your PPT explicitly associates fail-stop faults with Paxos/RAFT and Byzantine faults with PBFT/SMR protocols.

9. Consensus in Blockchain

Now connect this to blockchain.

A blockchain is a distributed ledger.

Multiple nodes maintain/participate in the network.

Therefore, they need to agree on:

  • Which transactions are valid
  • Which block is accepted
  • What the current ledger state is

Conceptually:

Transaction
     ↓
Validation
     ↓
Proposal
     ↓
CONSENSUS
     ↓
Accepted block/state
     ↓
Blockchain updated

Your PPT describes consensus as the process through which network peers determine which blockchain transactions are valid and which aren't.

10. Two Major Categories of Consensus

Your PPT divides consensus mechanisms into two major categories:

Category 1 — Proof-based / leader-election / lottery-based

Also called Nakamoto consensus in the PPT.

Category 2 — Byzantine Fault Tolerance (BFT)-based

11. Proof-Based / Leader-Election Consensus

The basic idea:

A mechanism determines which participant gets the right to propose the next block.

The PPT describes proof-based mechanisms as using proof of work, possession of authority, or tokens/stake to obtain the right to propose the next block.

Examples include:

  • Proof of Work
  • Proof of Stake
  • DPoS
  • PoET
  • PoD
  • PoI
  • PoC
  • PoA

12. BFT-Based Consensus

This is a more traditional distributed-systems approach.

Instead of massive computational work, nodes:

Exchange messages
       ↓
Multiple rounds/phases
       ↓
Receive sufficient messages
       ↓
Agreement

Your PPT describes BFT-based approaches as relying on rounds of votes/messages rather than compute-intensive operations such as Bitcoin's partial hash inversion.

Examples:

  • PBFT
  • Federated Byzantine consensus
  • FBA

13. PoW vs BFT

Your PPT gives an important comparison:

Proof/lottery based BFT based
Suitable for permissionless systems Often associated with permissioned/consortium systems
Leader selection through proof/lottery Rounds of voting/messages
Can scale to many nodes Performs well with limited number of nodes
PoW is slower BFT can provide faster agreement in appropriate settings
Bitcoin uses PoW PBFT is an example

The PPT notes that BFT mechanisms perform well with a limited number of nodes, while lottery-based PoW consensus can scale well but can be slow.

14. PROOF OF WORK — PoW ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is probably the most important consensus mechanism for your exam.

Bitcoin uses PoW.

Your PPT describes PoW as relying on proof that adequate computational resources have been spent before a value/block is accepted by the network.

15. Why PoW?

Imagine anyone can join Bitcoin.

How do we decide who gets to propose the next block?

We can't simply say:

"Everyone gets one vote."

Because someone could create:

1 computer
10 computers
100 computers
1,000,000 fake identities

This is called a Sybil attack.

PoW makes participation costly in terms of computation.

16. Basic PoW Idea

A miner constructs a candidate block and repeatedly changes a nonce until the resulting hash satisfies the network's difficulty requirement.

Simplified:

Block data + Nonce
        ↓
      Hash
        ↓
Does hash satisfy target?
      ↙       ↘
    NO         YES
     ↓          ↓
Change nonce   Block proposed
     ↓
 Try again

For example:

Nonce = 100 → Hash ❌
Nonce = 101 → Hash ❌
Nonce = 102 → Hash ❌
Nonce = 103 → Hash ❌
...
Nonce = 98273 → Hash ✅

The exact PoW target/difficulty details will become clearer when we study Bitcoin, but this is the concept you need now.

17. What Does the Miner Actually Prove?

The miner proves:

"I have spent computational resources to produce a valid solution."

That's why it's called:

Proof of Work

Not:

Proof that the miner worked hard manually 😂

It is a cryptographic/computational proof.

Your PPT explicitly describes PoW as proof that adequate computational resources have been spent.

18. PoW and Sybil Attacks

A Sybil attacker creates many fake identities.

Without a cost:

Attacker
 ↓
N1 N2 N3 N4 N5 N6 N7 ...

With PoW:

Each attempt requires computational resources.

Therefore, simply creating more identities doesn't automatically give unlimited influence.

Your PPT specifically mentions PoW as successful against collusion attacks such as Sybil attacks.

19. PoW Advantages

1. Open participation

Useful in permissionless blockchains.

2. Sybil resistance

Computational cost makes fake identities less useful.

3. Security

An attacker needs substantial computational power to consistently control the chain.

4. Proven model

Bitcoin successfully uses PoW.

20. PoW Disadvantages

The major issue:

High computational/energy requirement

Miners perform large numbers of calculations.

Therefore:

More computation
       ↓
More electricity/resources
       ↓
Higher cost

This is one reason alternative consensus mechanisms were developed.

21. PROOF OF STAKE — PoS ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Instead of asking:

"Who has done the most computational work?"

PoS asks:

"Who has a stake in the system?"

Your PPT describes PoS as selecting validators based on their stake.

22. Basic PoS Idea

Suppose:

Validator A → 100 coins
Validator B → 500 coins
Validator C → 1,000 coins

The protocol uses stake-related rules to select who can validate/propose the next block.

The exact selection mechanism differs between implementations.

Your PPT describes the historical version in terms of coin age, where the probability of proposing/signing increases with the amount of coins and how long they have remained unspent.

23. PoW vs PoS

PoW PoS
Based on computational work Based on stake
Uses computational resources Does not require PoW-style computation
Miners Validators
High energy use Lower energy requirement
Mining competition Stake-based selection
Bitcoin uses PoW PoS is used by various blockchain systems

Memory:

PoW = Work
PoS = Stake

24. Delegated Proof of Stake — DPoS

DPoS introduces delegation.

Imagine:

1000 token holders
       ↓
Vote
       ↓
Select delegates
       ↓
Delegates validate/propose blocks

Your PPT defines DPoS as allowing nodes with stake to delegate transaction validation to other nodes through voting. It gives BitShares as an example.

Simple difference:

PoS:
Stake → validator selection

DPoS:
Stakeholders → vote → delegates → validation

25. Proof of Elapsed Time — PoET

PoET uses time rather than computational competition.

Your PPT states that PoET was introduced by Intel in 2016 and uses a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) to provide randomness and safety in leader election through guaranteed wait times.

It requires Intel SGX for its security guarantee.

Concept

Node A → waits 5 seconds
Node B → waits 8 seconds
Node C → waits 3 seconds
             ↓
       C wins

The actual protocol uses a trusted execution environment to ensure the waiting process is trustworthy.

26. Proof of Deposit — PoD

Simple idea:

Participants must make a security deposit before they can participate in mining/proposing blocks.

Your PPT states exactly this and gives Tendermint as an example.

Think:

Deposit money
      ↓
Get participation rights
      ↓
Misbehave?
      ↓
Risk deposit

27. Proof of Importance — PoI

PoI considers the user's importance/activity rather than simply their balance.

Your PPT states that PoI monitors token usage and movement to establish a level of trust/importance.

Factors mentioned include:

  • Number of transactions
  • Size of transactions
  • Vested currency
  • Network activity

The protocol assigns an importance score, and a higher score increases the probability of being selected to harvest a block.

Example associated with your PPT:

NEM

28. Federated Consensus / Federated Byzantine Consensus

Here, nodes maintain a group of publicly trusted peers.

They propagate transactions validated by the majority of those trusted nodes.

Your PPT describes this mechanism in those terms.

Simple model:

Node
 ↓
Trusted peers
 ↓
Majority validation
 ↓
Accept transaction

This differs from completely open participation.

29. Reputation-Based Consensus

This uses reputation.

Your PPT describes a leader being elected based on the reputation built over time, with votes from other members.

Concept:

Good behavior
      ↓
Reputation increases
      ↓
More chance of leadership

30. PBFT — Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is extremely important because it connects directly to the Byzantine Generals Problem from Module 2.

Your PPT states that PBFT achieves State Machine Replication (SMR) and provides tolerance against Byzantine nodes.

31. Basic PBFT Idea

Suppose we have several nodes:

N1
N2
N3
N4

One node proposes something.

The nodes communicate through phases/rounds.

Conceptually:

Proposal
   ↓
Nodes exchange messages
   ↓
Verification / voting
   ↓
Sufficient agreement
   ↓
Commit

The key point is:

Even if some nodes are Byzantine, the honest nodes can still reach a consistent state, provided the protocol's assumptions are satisfied.

32. PBFT vs PoW

This is a very good comparison question.

PBFT PoW
BFT-based Proof-based
Uses communication/voting Uses computational work
Designed for Byzantine faults Provides consensus through computational competition
Generally suitable for known/limited participants Suitable for permissionless networks
No PoW-style mining computation Computationally intensive
Faster final agreement in suitable settings Can be slower

Your PPT categorizes BFT consensus as more traditional/permissioned and PoW as lottery-based/permissionless.

33. Proof of Activity — PoA

⚠️ Don't confuse this with Proof of Authority, which is also abbreviated PoA in your PPT.

Proof of Activity

It combines:

PoW + PoS

Your PPT says:

First stage → PoW
       ↓
Then → PoS

and notes that the PoW stage is used only initially, after which the mechanism switches to PoS with negligible energy consumption relative to PoW.

34. Proof of Capacity — PoC

Instead of computational power:

Hard disk space becomes the resource.

Your PPT calls this hard drive mining.

Basic idea:

Hard disk space
       ↓
Precompute/store hashes
       ↓
Use them during mining
       ↓
Participate in block creation

The PPT associates the introduction of this concept with BurstCoin.

35. Proof of Storage

The name gives you the basic idea:

Consensus/participation is based on demonstrating storage resources.

For your exam, if asked about the list of consensus mechanisms, remember this as a storage-oriented approach.

Your PPT lists Proof of Storage among the blockchain consensus mechanisms.

36. Proof of Authority — PoA ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Again:

PoA = Proof of Authority

Not Proof of Activity.

Here, known validators have authority to propose/validate blocks.

Your PPT says PoA uses the identity of participants as the stake.

Conceptually:

Known validators
      ↓
Identity + reputation
      ↓
Validate transactions
      ↓
Propose blocks

The PPT states that PoA is commonly used in private/consortium blockchains and mentions Clique and Aura as common PoA algorithms.

39. Why Consensus Is the Heart of Blockchain

Think about what happens if there is no consensus.

Node A:
Alice has ₹500

Node B:
Alice has ₹800

Node C:
Alice has ₹200

Now the distributed ledger isn't useful.

Consensus provides a way to converge on an accepted state:

N1 ─┐
N2 ─┤
N3 ─┼──→ CONSENSUS ──→ Accepted State
N4 ─┤
N5 ─┘

That's why your PPT calls consensus a crucial part of blockchain architecture.

⚠️ 42. VERY IMPORTANT EXAM TRAPS

Trap 1 — PoA has two meanings in your PPT

Your PPT contains:

Proof of Activity → PoA

and

Proof of Authority → PoA

So if the question says PoA, look at the context.

PoA
├── Proof of Activity
│      → PoW + PoS
│
└── Proof of Authority
       → Identity + known validators

Trap 2 — PoW isn't simply "finding a hash"

The important point is:

The miner demonstrates that computational resources have been spent to satisfy the network's PoW condition.

Trap 3 — PoS ≠ simply "richest person always wins"

Don't write that.

Say:

Validator selection is based on stake according to the protocol's selection mechanism.

Your PPT describes stake-based selection and, in its historical explanation, coin age.

Trap 4 — Consensus ≠ encryption

Consensus answers:

"Which state/transactions does the network accept?"

Cryptography answers things such as:

"How do we secure/authenticate/integrity-protect information?"

They work together but are different concepts.
