# 1. Public Key Cryptography ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Public-key cryptography is also called:

> **Asymmetric-key cryptography**

The key difference from symmetric cryptography is:

### Symmetric

```text
        SAME KEY
           ↓
Encryption ↔ Decryption
```

### Asymmetric

```text
       TWO DIFFERENT KEYS
              │
       ┌──────┴──────┐
       ↓             ↓
   PUBLIC KEY    PRIVATE KEY
```

Your PPT explicitly states that asymmetric cryptography uses **two separate keys: public and private**. 

---

# 2. Public Key vs Private Key ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

### Public Key

Can be distributed openly.

```text
Anyone can know it.
```

### Private Key

Must be kept secret by its owner.

```text
Only the owner should know it.
```

The fundamental idea is that the two keys are mathematically related, but knowing the public key should not allow an attacker to practically derive the private key.

---

# 3. Encryption Using Public-Key Cryptography

Suppose **Alice wants to send Bob a secret message**.

Bob has:

```text
Bob's public key
Bob's private key
```

Alice obtains Bob's **public key** and encrypts the message:

```text
Alice
  │
  │ Message
  ↓
Encrypt using Bob's PUBLIC KEY
  │
  ↓
Ciphertext
  │
  ↓
Bob
  │
  ↓
Decrypt using Bob's PRIVATE KEY
  │
  ↓
Original message
```

So:

$$
C = E(PU_B,M)
$$

and:

$$
M = D(PR_B,C)
$$

where:

* \(PU_B\) = Bob's public key
* \(PR_B\) = Bob's private key
* \(M\) = message
* \(C\) = ciphertext

### Exam sentence:

> **For confidentiality, the sender encrypts using the receiver's public key, and the receiver decrypts using their private key.**

# 5. RSA ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**RSA** is one of the most important public-key algorithms.

RSA is named after its inventors:

> **Rivest, Shamir and Adleman**

Your PPT calls RSA the most common public-key algorithm. 

### RSA can be used for:

* Encryption
* Decryption
* Digital signatures

---

# 6. RSA Basic Idea

RSA uses:

```text
PUBLIC KEY
   +
PRIVATE KEY
```

For encryption:

```text
Message
   ↓
RSA
   ↓
Encrypted message
```

The corresponding private key is then used for decryption.

The important mathematical basis behind RSA is the difficulty of **factoring large numbers**.

You don't need to get lost in the mathematics initially. For your exam, first remember:

> **RSA is an asymmetric/public-key cryptosystem based on the computational difficulty of factoring large integers.**

---

# 7. RSA Key Generation — Basic Steps ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The standard conceptual procedure is:

### Step 1 — Choose two large primes

$$
p,\ q
$$

### Step 2 — Calculate

$$
n=pq
$$

### Step 3 — Calculate Euler's totient

$$
\phi(n)=(p-1)(q-1)
$$

### Step 4 — Choose public exponent

Choose \(e\) such that:

$$
gcd(e,\phi(n))=1
$$

### Step 5 — Calculate private exponent

Find \(d\) such that:

$$
ed\equiv1\pmod{\phi(n)}
$$

### Public key

$$
(e,n)
$$

### Private key

$$
(d,n)
$$

---

# 8. RSA Encryption and Decryption ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

For message \(M\):

### Encryption

$$
C=M^e\bmod n
$$

### Decryption

$$
M=C^d\bmod n
$$

So remember:

```text
PUBLIC KEY  → encryption
PRIVATE KEY → decryption
```

for the confidentiality use case.

---

# 9. Small RSA Example

Let's use tiny numbers just to understand the mechanism.

Suppose:

$$
p=3,\quad q=11
$$

Then:

$$
n=3\times11=33
$$

and:

$$
\phi(n)=(3-1)(11-1)=20
$$

Choose:

$$
e=3
$$

because:

$$
gcd(3,20)=1
$$

Now find \(d\):

$$
3d\equiv1\pmod{20}
$$

Therefore:

$$
d=7
$$

because:

$$
3\times7=21\equiv1\pmod{20}
$$

So:

```text
Public key  = (3,33)
Private key = (7,33)
```

This is just a toy example; real RSA uses extremely large numbers.

# 10. Digital Signatures ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is **very important for blockchain**.

A digital signature provides evidence that a message was signed by the holder of a particular private key and allows verification using the corresponding public key.

Conceptually:

```text
              Alice
                │
             Message
                ↓
          Hash the message
                ↓
          Message Digest
                ↓
       Sign with PRIVATE KEY
                ↓
        DIGITAL SIGNATURE
```

The receiver can verify using Alice's public key.

Your PPT specifically includes **RSA Signature on the Message Digest** and an **Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Scheme**.  

---

# 11. Digital Signature Verification ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Suppose Alice sends:

```text
Message + Digital Signature
```

Bob:

```text
             Message
                ↓
              Hash
                ↓
          Digest A

Digital Signature
       ↓
Verify using
Alice's PUBLIC KEY
       ↓
    Digest B

Digest A == Digest B
       ↓
   Signature valid
```

If verification fails, something is wrong:

* Message may have been modified.
* Signature may not correspond to Alice's private key.

---

# 12. Digital Signature vs Encryption ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This distinction is **very important**.

### Encryption

Main purpose:

> **Confidentiality**

### Digital Signature

Main purposes include:

> **Authentication, integrity and non-repudiation**

For example:

```text
Encryption:

Public key → Encrypt
Private key → Decrypt
```

Whereas conceptually for a signature:

```text
Private key → Sign
Public key → Verify
```

### Easy memory:

> **Encryption protects the message from being read.**

> **Signature proves who signed it and helps detect modification.**

# 16. Homomorphic Encryption ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is an interesting and likely **5-mark theory question**.

Homomorphic encryption allows operations to be performed on **encrypted data without first decrypting it**.

Your PPT states that it enables complex mathematical operations on encrypted data without compromising the encryption. 

Conceptually:

```text
Plaintext:

5 + 10 = 15

Encrypt:

Enc(5) + Enc(10)

       ↓

Operation performed
WITHOUT decryption

       ↓

Enc(15)

       ↓ Decrypt

15
```

---

Supports an additive operation on encrypted data.

# 18. PPT Example of Homomorphic Encryption

Your PPT gives:


# 19. Signcryption ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This is another concept you should memorize.

**Signcryption** combines:

> **Digital signature + Encryption**

into one logical operation.

Your PPT states that signcryption provides the functions of a digital signature and encryption, including **unforgeability, authentication and non-repudiation**, with lower cost than independently signing and then encrypting. 

Conceptually:

### Traditional

```text
Message
   ↓
SIGN
   ↓
Signed message
   ↓
ENCRYPT
   ↓
Ciphertext
```

### Signcryption

```text
Message
   ↓
SIGNCRYPTION
   ↓
Signed + Encrypted message
```

### Important exam point:

$$
Cost(Signcryption)
<
Cost(Signature)+Cost(Encryption)
$$

according to the framing used in your PPT.

# 20. Secret Sharing ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Secret sharing means:

> A secret is divided among multiple entities, with each entity receiving a share.

For example:

```text
             SECRET
                │
         Split into shares
                │
       ┌────────┼────────┐
       ↓        ↓        ↓
    Share A  Share B  Share C
```

A certain number of shares can then be required to reconstruct the secret.

### Important property:

> **An individual share by itself does not reveal the secret.**

Your PPT explicitly describes reconstruction using all or some shares according to a threshold. 

---

# 21. Threshold Secret Sharing

Imagine a secret is divided among five people.

Suppose the rule is:

$$
3\text{ out of }5
$$

Then:

```text
A + B + C → Secret recovered
```

but:

```text
A + B → Not enough
```

This is called a **threshold** approach.

### Easy memory:

> **t-of-n = t shares required out of n total shares.**

# 22. Commitment Schemes ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A commitment scheme is useful when someone wants to:

> **Commit to a value now, but reveal it later.**

Think of it like putting a value inside a sealed envelope.

```text
       Alice chooses X
             ↓
          Commit
             ↓
      "I have committed"
             ↓
       Later reveal X
             ↓
          Verify
```

Two key ideas are commonly associated with commitment:

### Hiding

The commitment doesn't reveal the secret value.

### Binding

After committing, the sender shouldn't be able to change the committed value.

Your PPT lists **commitment schemes** among the cryptographic constructs covered in this unit.

# 23. Zero-Knowledge Proofs ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A **zero-knowledge proof (ZKP)** allows one party to prove that they know something or that a statement is true **without revealing the underlying secret information**.

Simple idea:

```text
Prover                         Verifier
   │                               │
   │ "I know the secret"           │
   │──────────────────────────────>│
   │                               │
   │        Proof                  │
   │──────────────────────────────>│
   │                               │
   │                        "Verified"
```

The verifier gains confidence that the prover knows the required information, but doesn't learn the secret itself.

Your PPT explicitly includes zero-knowledge proofs in its cryptographic constructs.

# 25. Threshold Signatures ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Your PPT also discusses **threshold signatures**.

Instead of requiring every participant to produce a separate signature, the private key is divided into multiple shares.

Each signer uses their share during signing.

The resulting process can produce:

> **One digital signature**

which the verifier checks using:

> **One public key**

Your PPT highlights that this can result in a smaller transaction and quicker verification. 

Conceptually:

```text
        Private Key
             │
       Split into shares
       /       |       \
      ↓        ↓        ↓
   Signer A Signer B Signer C
       \       |       /
        \      |      /
          Threshold
          Signature
               ↓
       One public key
               ↓
          Verification
```
